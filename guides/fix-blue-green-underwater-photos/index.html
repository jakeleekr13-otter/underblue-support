How to fix blue and green underwater photos

Most underwater photos come out blue or green. This is normal. Water changes the light before it reaches the camera.

This guide explains why it happens and how to fix it, during the dive and after it.

Why photos turn blue or green

Water absorbs red light first, then orange and yellow. Blue and green light travel further. So the deeper you go, and the further the subject is from the camera, the bluer the photo.

Green water often has plankton or algae in it. It makes the cast greener and the image softer.

Small particles in the water also reflect light back to the lens. This shows as haze or white specks, called backscatter.

During the dive: keep more color

  • Get close. Less water between the lens and the subject means more color.
  • Shoot in shallow water when you can. There is more red light near the surface.
  • Keep the sun behind you on sunny days.
  • Use a light or strobe for close subjects. Point it to the side, not straight ahead, to reduce backscatter.
  • A red filter on a phone or action camera can help in blue water at shallow depth.

After the dive: fix the color

Most photo editors can fix a blue or green cast. Work in this order:

  • White balance first. Use the eyedropper on something that should be white or gray, like sand or a tank.
  • If the photo is still blue, add warmth. If it is still green, add magenta.
  • Raise contrast a little. Water makes images flat.
  • Add saturation last, and only a little. Too much makes skin and coral look fake.
  • Shoot RAW if your camera can. RAW keeps more color to work with.

When a photo cannot be fully fixed

Some photos are too far gone. If the subject was far away in murky water, the red is almost gone. An editor or app can improve it, but not make it look like a photo taken close up. Next time, get closer.
